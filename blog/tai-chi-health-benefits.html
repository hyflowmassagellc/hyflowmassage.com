The Health Benefits of Tai Chi:
What the Research Says — and How to Start Gently

By Tina Zhang, CMT  ·  November 2026  ·  6 min read

Tai chi is often called "meditation in motion": slow, flowing movements done with relaxed, deep breathing and a calm, focused mind. It began as a Chinese martial art, but today millions of people practise it simply to feel steadier, looser and less stressed. It needs no equipment, can be done standing or seated, and is gentle enough for almost any age.

Tai chi is also part of two of our in-home programs. Here is what the research actually shows — including where the evidence is strong and where it is still early.

The Short Answer

The strongest evidence is for better balance and fewer falls in older adults and for less pain and stiffness in knee osteoarthritis. Smaller studies suggest tai chi may also help with low-back pain, fibromyalgia, sleep, mood, stress and quality of life in people living with heart or lung conditions or recovering from cancer treatment. It is generally very safe.

Where the Evidence Is Strongest

1 · Balance and fall prevention

Falls are a leading cause of injury in older adults. According to the U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH), a 2019 review found high-certainty evidence that tai chi may reduce the number of older people who fall by about 20 percent. The slow weight shifts, turns and single-leg moments train leg strength, posture and body awareness at the same time.

2 · Knee osteoarthritis

The American College of Rheumatology and Arthritis Foundation guidelines (2019) strongly recommend tai chi for knee osteoarthritis. Studies show improvements in pain, stiffness and everyday function — likely because it strengthens the muscles around the knee through gentle, low-impact movement.

Promising Benefits (Smaller Studies)

  • Low-back pain — tai chi alone or with physical therapy may reduce pain and improve daily function
  • Fibromyalgia — reduced pain, with some reports of better sleep, less fatigue and better mood
  • Stress, mood and sleep — slow breathing and movement help many people feel calmer; studies on sleep and depression are encouraging but mixed
  • Heart health — better quality of life and well-being in people with heart disease; possible small effects on blood pressure
  • COPD — improved exercise capacity and quality of life in early studies
  • Cancer survivors — improvements in fatigue, sleep and quality of life have been reported, though researchers say firm conclusions cannot yet be drawn

In other words: tai chi is not a cure for anything, and it does not replace medical care. But as gentle, whole-body movement with a strong safety record, it is one of the easiest healthy habits to start.

How to Start Safely

  • Start slowly. Five to ten minutes a day is enough at first. Movements should never hurt.
  • Sitting is fine. Seated tai chi works well if balance is a concern or you are recovering from illness or surgery.
  • Breathe naturally. Slow, relaxed breathing through the nose; never hold your breath.
  • Use support. Keep a sturdy chair or counter nearby for balance practice.
  • Check with your doctor first if you are pregnant, recovering from surgery, have joint replacements, osteoporosis, dizziness or a heart or lung condition.

What are the main health benefits of tai chi?

Is tai chi safe for seniors?

Yes. Tai chi has a strong safety record; reported side effects are usually minor aches. It can be done standing with support or seated. Older adults with balance problems, joint replacements or heart conditions should check with their doctor first.
