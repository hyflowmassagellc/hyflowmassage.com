Swollen Legs After a Flight?
Holiday Travel, Jet Lag & Massage in the Bay Area

By Tina Zhang, CMT  ·  November 2026  ·  7 min read

Thanksgiving and the December holidays are the busiest travel weeks of the year at SFO and San José. Whether you are flying home to see family or hosting relatives who just landed from Asia or the East Coast, a familiar complaint follows long flights: tight, puffy ankles, shoes that suddenly feel small, a stiff neck and a body clock that is wide awake at 3 AM.

Here is what causes that post-flight swelling, which signs mean you should call a doctor instead of booking a massage, a few simple jet-lag habits that actually help, and how we can bring recovery massage to your home or hotel.

The Short Answer

Mild swelling in both feet and ankles after a long flight is common and usually fades within a day once you are moving. Swelling in one leg, with pain, warmth or redness — or any shortness of breath or chest pain — needs medical care right away, not massage. For ordinary travel stiffness, walking, water, daylight and a gentle massage can help you feel like yourself again. Why Feet and Ankles Swell on a Plane

When you sit for hours with your feet on the floor, the calf muscles that normally squeeze blood and fluid back up toward the heart are barely working. According to Mayo Clinic, inactivity during long flights lets blood pool in the leg veins, and the seated position raises pressure in those veins, so fluid moves into the surrounding tissue of the feet and ankles. Cramped seats, salty airline meals, alcohol and not drinking enough water can make it feel worse.

For most healthy people this kind of swelling is harmless and settles on its own once you start walking around. Mayo Clinic's tips are simple:

  • Move every hour or so. Walk the aisle when it is safe to do so.
  • Work your calves in your seat. Flex and point your feet, circle your ankles and press your heels and toes into the floor.
  • Dress loosely. Avoid tight waistbands, socks and shoes that dig in.
  • Drink water and go easy on alcohol.

When Swelling Is Not "Just Travel": Blood Clot Warning Signs

Long trips also carry a small but real risk of a blood clot in a deep leg vein (deep vein thrombosis, or DVT). The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that risk rises on journeys longer than about four hours, and is higher for people with a previous clot, recent surgery or injury, pregnancy or recent childbirth, cancer, estrogen-containing medicines, obesity or limited mobility.

See a doctor promptly if, after travel, you notice:

  • Swelling in one leg, ankle or foot rather than both
  • Pain, cramping or tenderness in the calf or thigh
  • Warm, red or discoloured skin on the leg
  • Swelling that does not improve after a few days

Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain that is worse when you breathe in, a fast heartbeat, or coughing up blood — these can be signs of a clot that has travelled to the lungs (pulmonary embolism).

If any of these apply, please do not book a massage. Massage should never be done on a leg where a clot is suspected. If you are at higher risk, ask your doctor before your trip whether graduated compression stockings or other precautions make sense for you.

Five Jet-Lag Habits That Actually Help

Jet lag happens when your internal clock is still on your departure time zone. Mayo Clinic notes it is usually worse flying east and can take several days to fade. A few habits help your body catch up:

  • Get daylight at the right time. Morning light helps after flying east (for example, from Asia back to California); late-afternoon light helps after flying west.
  • Switch to local time right away for meals and bedtime, and keep naps short (20–30 minutes) and early.
  • Stay hydrated and limit alcohol and late-day caffeine, which both disturb sleep.
  • Move gently. A walk outside gives you daylight and gets your legs moving at the same time.
  • Wind down in the evening with a warm shower, dim lights and slow breathing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do my feet and ankles swell after a long flight?

Sitting still for hours with your feet down lets blood and fluid pool in the lower legs. According to Mayo Clinic, this kind of mild swelling during air travel is common and usually harmless, and it typically goes away within a day or so once you are moving again.

When is leg swelling after a flight a reason to see a doctor?

Get medical care if swelling is in one leg only, comes with pain, tenderness, warmth or redness, or lasts more than a few days. Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain or a racing heartbeat. These can be signs of a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism), which the CDC notes is more likely on trips longer than about four hours.

Is it safe to get a massage after a long flight?

For most healthy travelers, yes. Massage is not appropriate on a leg where a blood clot is suspected, and people with a history of clots, recent surgery, pregnancy, heart or kidney conditions or bleeding disorders should check with their doctor first. Tell your therapist about any of these before the session.

Can massage cure jet lag?

No. Jet lag resets mainly through light, sleep timing and time itself. A relaxing massage can ease stiffness from travel and help you unwind, which many people find makes it easier to rest on the new schedule, but it is not a treatment for jet lag.
